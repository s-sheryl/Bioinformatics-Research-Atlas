A critical analysis of Argentieri et al. (2024), *"Proteomic Aging Clock Predicts Mortality and Risk of Common Age-Related Diseases in Diverse Populations"* (*Nature Medicine*), focused on what the model predicts, how it was validated, and how far its biological and clinical interpretation can reasonably extend.

## Overview

This repository contains a critical analysis of a 2024 *Nature Medicine* paper that develops a proteomic aging clock using 2,897 plasma proteins measured in the UK Biobank. The authors used machine learning to select 204 age-predictive proteins and tested the model in the China Kadoorie Biobank and FinnGen. They then examined whether ProtAgeGap—the difference between a participant's proteomic age and chronological age—was associated with aging-related traits, chronic diseases, multimorbidity, and mortality.

I picked this paper because it brings together several areas I want to understand better: proteomics, machine learning, biomarker development, and the interpretation of predictive models in health research. The model predicts chronological age accurately, including in two external cohorts, but that raises a more difficult question: does accurate age prediction mean that the model is measuring biological aging, or is it mainly detecting a strong molecular signal related to chronological age?

The analysis is not a re-implementation or a new experiment. It is a close reading of the study's design, modelling choices, validation strategy, and interpretation. Readers will get an explanation of the LightGBM, Boruta, and SHAP workflow, an assessment of the evidence behind ProtAgeGap, a discussion of what was and was not externally validated, and a set of follow-up analyses that could test the model's stability and clinical value more directly.

## Highlights

The analysis follows the study from model development to biological interpretation. It looks at the separation of the UK Biobank training and holdout sets, the model's performance in CKB and FinnGen, the reduction from 2,897 proteins to a 204-protein clock, and the use of ProtAgeGap in the downstream phenotype and disease analyses.

The central criticism is not that the clock lacks value. Its chronological-age prediction is strong, and the reported outcome associations make it useful for research and hypothesis generation. The issue is that different results support different levels of interpretation. Accurate age prediction does not by itself prove that ProtAgeGap measures one underlying biological-aging process, and an adjusted association with disease does not show how much ProtAgeGap improves individual risk prediction beyond established clinical information. In the same way, SHAP importance and STRING coexpression evidence provide useful biological context, but they do not establish causal mechanisms.

The analysis also proposes several follow-up directions I would be interested in exploring, including residual diagnostics across age groups, comparison of clinical models with and without ProtAgeGap, resampled feature selection, external replication of disease associations, and validation on another proteomics platform.

## Key Takeaways

- A model can predict chronological age accurately without necessarily measuring biological age as a single underlying process.
- Validation in CKB and FinnGen shows that the age-prediction model transports across different cohorts, but most disease and mortality associations were evaluated in UK Biobank and need separate external replication.
- ProtAgeGap is associated with several health outcomes after adjustment, but association is not the same as improved clinical prediction or causation.
- Boruta and SHAP identify proteins that are useful to the fitted model; they do not show that those proteins cause aging or disease.
- STRING coexpression relationships add biological context, but they are not experimental confirmation of direct protein interactions.
- Residual calibration, feature-selection stability, and comparisons with established clinical models would help clarify the clock's value beyond chronological-age prediction.

## Future Work

Based on the limitations and extensions discussed in the analysis, useful next steps would include:

- Comparing a baseline clinical model with the same model plus ProtAgeGap using discrimination and calibration measures
- Examining ProtAgeGap across chronological-age groups to check for residual age bias
- Repeating protein selection across bootstrap samples and random seeds to measure feature stability
- Replicating disease and mortality associations in independent cohorts with sufficient follow-up and event counts
- Testing whether the 20-protein model retains its performance on another proteomics platform
- Investigating highly ranked proteins using longitudinal, genetic, or experimental evidence
